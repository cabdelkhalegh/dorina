# Workshop Exercises and Handouts — Dorina Salman

**Purpose:** This is the complete, ready-to-print facilitation toolkit for Dorina's workplace-wellbeing workshops. It contains verbatim opening and closing scripts, seven fully written core exercises (facilitator cards plus participant handout text), two manager-session add-ons, and all printable handout masters. It is the hands-on companion to `WORKSHOP_SYSTEM_BLUEPRINT.md` (which defines the session formats that use these exercises) and operates under the safety rules of `RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md`. Everything here is educational and non-clinical: Dorina teaches practical mental health *management* — stress literacy, boundaries, workload structure, and help-seeking as a skill — and never diagnoses, treats, or assesses anyone.

---

## 1. How to Use This Toolkit

Each exercise is self-contained: a **facilitator card** (what Dorina does and says) and a **participant handout** (full text, ready to lay out and print). Pick exercises according to the session format defined in `WORKSHOP_SYSTEM_BLUEPRINT.md`; the DESK Method (proposed) is the spine that connects them.

### 1.1 Exercise ↔ DESK mapping

| # | Exercise | DESK letter | Core skill taught | Time | Best for |
|---|----------|-------------|-------------------|------|----------|
| 1 | Stress Signal Map | **D — Detect** | Noticing early overload signals without stigma | 20 min | All-staff sessions |
| 2 | Workload X-Ray | **E — Establish** | Making invisible workload visible | 25 min | All-staff and team sessions |
| 3 | Boundary Scripts Lab | **E — Establish** | Saying no respectfully, in Arabic and English | 30 min | All-staff sessions |

| 6 | The Friday 15 Weekly Review | **K — Keep** | A sustainable weekly self-check ritual | 15 min teach + take-home | Closing block of any session |
| 7 | Energy Ledger | **K — Keep** | Tracking drains and gains; leisure and movement habits | 10 min teach + one-week take-home | Closing block; follow-up sessions |

| M2 | Team Workload Weather Report | **D / E** | A recurring team check-in on load, not feelings | 15 min teach | Manager session (W2) only |

### 1.2 Safety rules that apply to every exercise (non-negotiable)

1. **Participation is always voluntary.** Every exercise is an invitation, never a requirement.
2. **The pass rule.** Anyone may say "pass" on any question, pairing, or share-back — at any point, without giving a reason, and without being asked why. Dorina states this rule aloud before every exercise that involves speaking.
3. **No personal-trauma prompts.** No exercise ever asks participants to share personal health history, past crises, family matters, or emotionally painful memories. All prompts stay at the level of *work situations, workload, habits, and routines*.
4. **The facilitator never interprets anyone's feelings.** Dorina reflects back what people *say about their work*, never what she thinks they *feel or "really" mean*. No labeling, no analysis, no armchair diagnosis — ever.
5. **Nothing here is a screening tool.** No exercise produces a score, a category, or a "result" about anyone's mental state. Signals of overload are discussed as normal human responses to work conditions, not symptoms.
6. **Disclosure protocol.** If a participant discloses distress or asks for personal help, Dorina follows the disclosure and referral steps in `RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md` — acknowledge, keep it private, signpost to qualified help, never probe.
7. **The footer disclaimer block (Section 8) appears on every printed handout.** No exceptions.

## 2. Opening Script (Verbatim, ~2 Minutes)

Dorina delivers this at the start of every session, before any exercise. Read it as written until it is memorized; the safety beats are not optional.

> "Good morning, everyone — welcome, and thank you for giving this hour to yourselves.
>
> My name is Dorina Salman. I spent more than twelve years in administrative and executive-assistant roles, so I know from the inside what deadlines, inboxes, and 'quick favors' at 7 p.m. actually feel like. Today I'm here as a **workplace wellbeing educator**. Let me be very clear about what that means: **I am an educator, not a therapist.** I don't diagnose anyone, I don't treat anyone, and I won't ask anyone to share anything personal about their health. What I *will* do is teach practical, learnable skills for managing stress, workload, and boundaries at work.
>
> This session is education, not therapy. We'll talk about work — workload, routines, boundaries, and how to ask for support — not about anyone's private life or medical history.
>
> Three ground rules keep this room safe. **One:** everything is voluntary — every exercise is an invitation. **Two:** you can say 'pass' at any moment, on anything, and no one — including me — will ask you why. **Three:** what colleagues share here stays here.
>
> One more important thing. If anything we discuss today touches something you're personally struggling with, please talk to a qualified professional — I'll show you at the end exactly where to find that kind of support, and there's a card in your handout pack with the details. Asking for that help is a skill and a strength, and it belongs with people trained to give it.
>
> That's the frame. Let's begin."

### 4.1 Exercise 1 — Stress Signal Map (D — Detect)

**Facilitator card**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Objective | Build stress literacy: participants learn to notice their own early overload signals at work — in body, behavior, and thinking — and to name them without stigma. |
| DESK letter | D — Detect |
| Time | 20 minutes |
| Group size | Any; works from 6 to 60 |
| Materials | Handout 4.1, pens; flipchart with three columns labeled Body / Behavior / Thinking |

**Step-by-step facilitation script:**

1. **Frame (2 min).** Say: *"The World Health Organization recommends mental health literacy for workers — plain knowledge that improves understanding and reduces stigma. That's what this exercise is: literacy, not diagnosis. Overload has early signals, the way a car has a fuel light. We're going to learn to read our own dashboard. This is a map of signals — it is not a checklist of symptoms, it produces no score, and it says nothing about anyone's health."*
2. **Demonstrate (3 min).** On the flipchart, offer neutral, generic examples in each column. Say: *"For example — Body: tense shoulders by mid-afternoon. Behavior: re-reading the same email three times, skipping lunch to 'save time'. Thinking: everything starts to feel equally urgent."* Use only work-flavored, everyday examples.
3. **Individual work (7 min).** Say: *"On your handout, privately, note two or three of your own early signals in each column. This paper is yours — no one will collect it, and no one will read it."*
4. **Optional pair share (4 min).** Say: *"If you'd like, tell a partner ONE signal you're comfortable sharing — something work-related, like 'I stop taking my lunch break.' And remember the pass rule: staying silent is completely fine."*
5. **Debrief (4 min).** Ask the debrief questions below to the room; take only volunteered answers.

**Debrief questions:**
- "What surprised you about your own list?"
- "Which signal shows up *earliest* for you — the fuel light, not the empty tank?"
- "What's one thing you could do the same day you notice your earliest signal?"

**Safety notes / what NOT to do:**
- Never turn this into a symptom inventory: no counting signals, no thresholds, no "if you ticked more than X." The map has no score by design.
- Never comment on any individual's signals ("that sounds like anxiety" is forbidden). Reflect the work situation only.
- If someone shares something clearly personal or distressing, thank them briefly, do not probe, and follow `RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md`.
- Do not go around the room; volunteers only.

**Low-tech variant:** No flipchart? Draw the three columns on any whiteboard or dictate them; participants use blank paper folded in thirds.

**Participant handout — full text (Handout 4.1):**

> ### My Stress Signal Map
> *Private working sheet — yours to keep. No one collects or reads this.*
>
> Everyone's system sends early signals when work load runs hot — like a fuel light on a dashboard. Learning to read your own signals early is a skill the WHO calls mental health literacy: it improves understanding and reduces stigma. **These are signals of overload, not symptoms of illness. This sheet has no score and diagnoses nothing.**
>
> | My early signals at work | Examples (generic) | Mine |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Body** | Tight shoulders; headaches by 4 p.m.; running on coffee | ____________________ |
> | **Behavior** | Skipping lunch; re-reading emails; snapping at small requests; working late "just this once," every week | ____________________ |
> | **Thinking** | Everything feels urgent; hard to start simple tasks; replaying work conversations at night | ____________________ |
>
> **My earliest signal — the fuel light, not the empty tank:** ____________________
>
> **One small action I can take the same day I notice it:** ____________________
>
> *A useful frame:* signals usually point at **conditions** — workload, pace, hours, control — more than at you. When a signal repeats, the question is not "what's wrong with me?" but "what in my week needs adjusting?" The next exercises show you how.

### 4.2 Exercise 2 — Workload X-Ray (E — Establish)

**Facilitator card**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Objective | Make invisible workload visible: participants inventory one real week of tasks, then examine it through the WHO's workplace risk lens (workload, pace, hours, control). |
| DESK letter | E — Establish |
| Time | 25 minutes |
| Group size | Any; individual work with optional pairs |
| Materials | Handout 4.2, pens |

**Step-by-step facilitation script:**

1. **Frame (3 min).** Say: *"The WHO names specific workplace risks to mental health: excessive workloads and work pace, understaffing, long or inflexible hours, and lack of control over your work. Notice something — those are all* conditions*, not character flaws. But conditions are hard to fix while they're invisible. Administrative work especially runs on invisible tasks: the follow-ups, the reminders, the 'can you just.' Today we X-ray one week."*
2. **Task inventory (8 min).** Say: *"Column one: list everything you actually did last week — including the invisible items. The five-minute favors. The chasing. The things nobody sees. Don't rank, just dump."*
3. **Apply the risk lens (7 min).** Say: *"Now mark each task with one or more letters. W if the sheer amount is the issue. P if the speed demanded is the issue. H if it lands outside reasonable hours. C if you had no say in when or how it arrived. Some tasks get no letter — that's fine, that's healthy work."*
4. **Find the pattern (4 min).** Say: *"Look at your letters. Which letter appears most? That's your week's dominant pressure — and each pressure has a different fix. Too much W is a prioritization and delegation conversation. Lots of H is a boundaries conversation — which is exactly our next exercise."*
5. **Debrief (3 min).** Volunteers only.

**Debrief questions:**
- "How much of your list would your manager be surprised to see?"
- "Which letter dominated — and did that surprise you?"
- "What's ONE item you could make visible to someone this week — not complain about, just make visible?"

**Safety notes / what NOT to do:**
- Keep the frame on conditions, not coping. This exercise never asks how people *feel* about tasks — only what the tasks *are*.
- Do not let the debrief become a complaint session about named managers or colleagues; redirect to patterns ("lots of H-tasks") rather than people.
- Do not collect the sheets.

**Low-tech variant:** Blank paper with a hand-drawn two-column table; letters written in the margin.

**Participant handout — full text (Handout 4.2):**

> ### Workload X-Ray
> *Private working sheet — make one real week visible.*
>
> The WHO identifies specific workplace risks to mental health: excessive **W**orkload, unsustainable **P**ace, long or inflexible **H**ours, and lack of **C**ontrol. These are conditions of work — and conditions can be adjusted, but only once they are visible.
>
> **Step 1 — Inventory.** List everything you did last week, especially the invisible tasks (follow-ups, reminders, "quick favors," fixing others' formatting, being the office memory).
>
> | Task (visible or invisible) | Risk letters (W / P / H / C) |
> |---|---|
> | | |
> | | |
> | | |
> | | |
> | | |
> | | |
> | | |
> | | |
>
> **Step 2 — Read your X-ray.**
> - Most frequent letter this week: ______
> - **W dominates →** the conversation to have is about priorities: "Which of these should come first?"
> - **P dominates →** the conversation is about sequencing: "What's the real deadline on each?"
> - **H dominates →** the conversation is about boundaries — see the Boundary Scripts handout.
> - **C dominates →** the conversation is about planning: "Can I get these requests earlier / batched?"
>
> **One item I will make visible to my manager or team this week:** ____________________

### 4.3 Exercise 3 — Boundary Scripts Lab (E — Establish)

**Facilitator card**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Objective | Participants write and rehearse respectful, culturally natural boundary sentences for real Lebanese workplace situations — in Arabic and English — so the words are ready before the moment arrives. |
| DESK letter | E — Establish |
| Time | 30 minutes |
| Group size | Pairs; any room size |
| Materials | Handout 4.3, pens |

**Step-by-step facilitation script:**

1. **Frame (4 min).** Say: *"A boundary is not a wall and it is not a fight — it's a clear sentence, said kindly, that protects your capacity to keep doing good work. In our workplace culture, warmth matters: we don't say a cold 'no.' The formula we'll practice is* **acknowledge + limit + alternative**: *I hear you, here's my limit, here's what I* can *do. The magic is preparing the sentence before you need it — because at 7 p.m., tired, on the spot, nobody improvises well."*
2. **Model (4 min).** Read example script 1 (below) aloud in both languages. Point out the three parts.
3. **Write (8 min).** Say: *"Pick the scenario on your handout closest to your real life — or write your own work scenario. Draft your sentence in whichever language you'd actually use. Use the formula."*
4. **Pair practice (10 min).** Say: *"In pairs: one of you makes the request, the other delivers the boundary sentence. Then swap. Requester — be realistic, not cruel. And the pass rule applies: you can write without practicing aloud."*
5. **Debrief (4 min).**

**Debrief questions:**
- "How did the sentence feel out loud compared to in your head?"
- "Which of the three parts — acknowledge, limit, alternative — is hardest for you?"
- "Where will you keep your sentence so it's ready when you need it?"

**Safety notes / what NOT to do:**
- Scenarios stay professional: workload, hours, scope. Never set up scenarios involving harassment, discrimination, or abusive behavior — those need HR and formal channels, not a phrasing exercise; say so if asked, and point to workplace policy.
- Do not force anyone to role-play; writing alone is a complete version of this exercise.
- Do not critique anyone's delivery style, accent, or tone.

**Low-tech variant:** No handout? Write the three-part formula on the board and dictate the four scenarios.

**Participant handout — full text (Handout 4.3):**

> ### Boundary Scripts Lab
> *The formula: Acknowledge + Limit + Alternative — كلمة طيّبة، حدّ واضح، وبديل عملي (a kind word, a clear limit, a practical alternative).*
>
> **Script 1 — Declining the 7 p.m. "quick favor"**
> - **EN:** "I appreciate that this is important — and I've finished for today. I'll make it the first thing I do tomorrow morning, and you'll have it by 10."
> - **AR:** «بقدّر إنّو الموضوع مهم — بس أنا خلّصت دوامي اليوم. بكرا الصبح أوّل شي رح اشتغل عليه، وبيكون عندك قبل العشرة.»
>
> **Script 2 — Protecting your lunch break**
> - **EN:** "I have a commitment from 1:00 to 1:30. I'm fully available right after — shall we say 1:30?"
> - **AR:** «عندي التزام من الوحدة للوحدة ونص. بعدها فاضية تماماً — منحكي الساعة وحدة ونص؟»
> *(Your lunch IS a commitment. You don't have to say to whom.)*
>
> **Script 3 — Renegotiating a deadline**
> - **EN:** "I want to give this the quality it deserves. With my current list, I can deliver it Thursday — or, if Wednesday is essential, tell me which of my other tasks moves."
> - **AR:** «بدّي قدّم هالشغل بالجودة يلّي بيستاهلها. مع المهام يلّي عندي حالياً، فيني سلّمه الخميس — وإذا الأربعا ضروري، خبّرني أيّا مهمة تانية منأجّلها.»
>
> **Script 4 — Saying no to scope creep**
> - **EN:** "Happy to help with that. Since it's outside what we agreed, let's decide together what comes off my plate to make room for it."
> - **AR:** «بكل سرور بساعد. بس بما إنّو هالشي خارج يلّي اتفقنا عليه، خلّينا نقرّر سوا شو منأجّل من مهامي حتى يصير في مجال.»
>
> **Now write yours:**
> - My real scenario: ____________________
> - Acknowledge: ____________________
> - Limit: ____________________
> - Alternative: ____________________
>
> *Keep your sentence where you'll find it in the moment — a sticky note, your phone, your desk drawer. Boundaries are not walls; they are the maintenance schedule that keeps you reliable.*

### 4.4 Exercise 4 — White-Space Audit (E / K)

**Facilitator card**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Objective | Participants audit a real week of their calendar to find recovery gaps ("white space"), then protect at least two of them deliberately. |
| DESK letters | E — Establish / K — Keep |
| Time | 20 minutes |
| Group size | Any; individual work |
| Materials | Handout 4.4, pens; participants' own calendars (phone or paper) |

**Step-by-step facilitation script:**

1. **Frame (3 min).** Say: *"Rest is not what's left over after work — it's a system you design. The WHO points to long or inflexible hours as a genuine workplace risk, and to leisure and physical activity as part of the answer. White space is any gap in your week where nothing is scheduled and nothing is owed. Most of us have some — and we let it get eaten, five minutes at a time."*
2. **Map (7 min).** Say: *"Open last week in your calendar. On the handout grid, shade the blocks that were truly free — no meetings, no tasks, no 'catching up.' Be honest: scrolling through work emails on the sofa is not white space."*
3. **Diagnose (4 min).** Say: *"Now answer the three questions on the sheet: how much white space survived, what usually eats it, and when it disappears — is it always evenings? Always the same day?"*
4. **Protect (4 min).** Say: *"Choose two blocks in the coming week and book them with yourself, by name — 'walk,' 'lunch away from the desk,' 'nothing.' A named block survives; a blank one gets taken."*
5. **Debrief (2 min).** One volunteered insight or two.

**Debrief questions:**
- "What ate most of your white space — others' requests, or your own habits?"
- "What will you *call* your protected blocks? Named blocks survive."

**Safety notes / what NOT to do:**
- Never comment on how little white space anyone has; no comparisons between participants.
- Keep it practical, not moral — no guilt framing about rest ("you *should*…").
- If someone's schedule includes caregiving or a second job, acknowledge that white space can be small and still count; never suggest their life design is wrong.

**Low-tech variant:** Hand-draw a 7-day × 3-band (morning / afternoon / evening) grid on blank paper.

**Participant handout — full text (Handout 4.4):**

> ### White-Space Audit
> *White space = time where nothing is scheduled and nothing is owed. It is where recovery happens.*
>
> **Step 1 — Map last week.** Shade only the blocks that were genuinely free (no work, no "catching up," no half-working on the sofa).
>
> | | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat | Sun |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Morning | | | | | | | |
> | Afternoon | | | | | | | |
> | Evening | | | | | | | |
>
> **Step 2 — Diagnose.**
> - Blocks of true white space last week: ______
> - What usually eats my white space: ____________________
> - When it disappears most (day/time pattern): ____________________
>
> **Step 3 — Protect two blocks this coming week.** Give each a name — named blocks survive.
> - Block 1: Day ______ Time ______ Name: ____________________
> - Block 2: Day ______ Time ______ Name: ____________________
>
> *Good names: "walk," "lunch away from my desk," "coffee with no phone," "nothing — on purpose." Rest as a system, not a leftover — that is the K in DESK.*

### 4.5 Exercise 5 — Help-Seeking Compass (S — Support)

**Facilitator card**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Objective | Participants map their own support options in three rings — self-management, workplace support, professional help — learn what each ring is *for*, and leave knowing where qualified help lives. Frames help-seeking explicitly as a skill and a strength. |
| DESK letter | S — Support |
| Time | 20 minutes |
| Group size | Any; individual work, no sharing required |
| Materials | Handout 4.5, pens; referral signposting cards (Section 7.2) ready to distribute |

**Step-by-step facilitation script:**

1. **Frame (4 min).** Say: *"Help-seeking is a skill — you can be good at it, and today we practice the first step: knowing your map before you need it. Three rings. Ring one, self-management: the routines and skills in this workshop. Ring two, workplace support: a trusted colleague or a manager — good for workload adjustments, priorities, and not carrying things alone. Ring three, professional support: qualified practitioners — the right address when distress is persistent, heavy, or beyond what routines and conversations at work can carry. The WHO is clear that mental health is a basic human right and that real care belongs in qualified pathways. Moving to ring three is not failure. It's the same skill as calling an accountant for taxes: the strong move is knowing which problems deserve a professional."*
2. **Individual mapping (8 min).** Say: *"Fill in your own compass. Ring two: write initials, not names, of one or two people at work you could actually talk to about workload. Ring three: you don't need to know a specific professional today — write where you would* look. *In Lebanon, the Ministry of Public Health's National Mental Health Programme publishes awareness and support materials, and your referral card points there."*
3. **Distribute the referral card (3 min).** Hand out Section 7.2 cards. Say: *"This card lives in your wallet or on your phone. You may never need it. Someone near you might — and you'll be the person who knew where to point."*
4. **Debrief (5 min).** Volunteers only; keep answers at the level of the *map*, not personal situations.

**Debrief questions:**
- "Which ring was easiest to fill in? Which was emptiest?"
- "What would make it easier to use ring two — to actually raise workload with someone?"
- "Ring three: where would you look first, now that you've seen the card?"

**Safety notes / what NOT to do:**
- Never ask anyone whether they *need* ring three, have used it, or plan to. The map is hypothetical and private.
- Never recommend a specific named practitioner; signpost only to qualified pathways as on the referral card.
- If a participant asks "do you think I need therapy?", the answer is verbatim: *"That's exactly the kind of question a qualified professional can answer with you — and asking it is a strength. Your card shows where to start."* Then follow `RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md`.

**Low-tech variant:** Draw three concentric circles on blank paper.

**Participant handout — full text (Handout 4.5):**

> ### My Help-Seeking Compass
> *Private sheet. Help-seeking is a skill — and knowing your map before you need it is the first move. طلب المساعدة مهارة وقوة، مش ضعف — asking for help is a skill and a strength, not a weakness.*
>
> **Ring 1 — Self-management (what I do myself).**
> For: everyday stress, workload structure, routines, rest, boundaries.
> My three most reliable tools (from today or my own life): 1) ______________ 2) ______________ 3) ______________
>
> **Ring 2 — Workplace support (who I can talk to at work).**
> For: workload adjustments, priorities, making the invisible visible, not carrying things alone.
> *Not for:* diagnosis or treatment — colleagues and managers are allies, not clinicians.
> One or two people (initials only): ______________
> What I would ask for (an adjustment, not a rescue): ____________________
>
> **Ring 3 — Professional support (qualified help).**
> For: distress that is persistent, heavy, or bigger than routines and work conversations can carry. Mental health is a basic human right, and qualified care is where real treatment belongs.
> Where I would look first: the Ministry of Public Health's **National Mental Health Programme** publishes awareness and support material — see your referral card, which also lists the support helpline and emergency guidance.
>
> **The rule of the compass:** you never need to be in crisis to use ring 3, and using it never means rings 1 and 2 failed. Strong professionals use other professionals.

### 4.6 Exercise 6 — The Friday 15 Weekly Review (K — Keep)

**Facilitator card**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Objective | Install a 15-minute weekly self-review ritual: look back at load, flag next week's risks, plan white space, and name one ask-for-help item. This is the maintenance habit that keeps the whole DESK Method running. |
| DESK letter | K — Keep |
| Time | 15 minutes to teach and run once live; then weekly take-home |
| Group size | Any; individual |
| Materials | Handout 4.6 (the template — print several copies per person), pens |

**Step-by-step facilitation script:**

1. **Frame (3 min).** Say: *"Everything you learned today decays without maintenance — that's not a flaw in you, it's how habits work. The fix is a standing appointment with yourself: fifteen minutes, once a week, ideally Friday before you close the laptop. Four questions, every week, same order. Weekly reviews are how you catch the fuel light in review rather than on the highway."*
2. **Run it live (9 min).** Say: *"Let's do this week's review right now, together, silently. Question one: what was this week's load actually like — heavier, lighter, or as planned? Two minutes."* Continue through all four questions, timing each: *"Question two: looking at next week, where's the risk — which day, which task, which letter from your X-ray? … Question three: where's your white space next week — name two blocks. … Question four: what is ONE thing you'll ask someone for — help, clarity, a deadline, a priority call? One ask. Small is fine."*
3. **Anchor it (3 min).** Say: *"Now open your calendar and create the recurring appointment: Friday, fifteen minutes, titled 'Friday 15.' If Friday doesn't fit your week, pick your own day — the ritual matters more than the day."*

**Debrief questions:**
- "Which of the four questions was hardest to answer?"
- "What will realistically try to steal this slot — and what's your boundary sentence for it?"

**Safety notes / what NOT to do:**
- The review looks at *load and plans*, never mood-tracking or symptom-tracking. Do not add rating scales of any kind.
- Never ask participants to share their answers; the live run is silent.

**Low-tech variant:** The four questions on an index card taped inside a notebook.

**Participant handout — full text (Handout 4.6 — print multiple copies):**

> ### The Friday 15 — Weekly Review Template
> *15 minutes. Same four questions, every week. Week of: ____________*
>
> **1. LOOK BACK — this week's load (4 min).**
> Heavier / lighter / as planned? What made it so? Which invisible tasks appeared?
> ____________________
>
> **2. FLAG — next week's risk (4 min).**
> Which day looks heaviest? Which task carries a W / P / H / C risk? What would make it lighter *if I raised it now*?
> ____________________
>
> **3. PROTECT — white space (3 min).**
> Two named blocks for next week:
> Block 1: ______________ Block 2: ______________
>
> **4. ASK — one ask-for-help item (4 min).**
> One thing I will ask someone for next week (clarity, a priority call, a hand, a deadline shift) — and who:
> ____________________
>
> *Done. Close the laptop. The review is the system; the system is what keeps you well-resourced — that's the K in DESK.*

### 4.7 Exercise 7 — Energy Ledger (K — Keep)

**Facilitator card**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Objective | Over one week, participants track what drains and what restores their energy — with deliberate attention to leisure and physical activity, which the WHO includes among recommended individual interventions — and then adjust one habit. |
| DESK letter | K — Keep |
| Time | 10 minutes to teach; one-week take-home; 10-minute debrief if a follow-up session exists |
| Group size | Any; individual |
| Materials | Handout 4.7 (the printable ledger), pens |

**Step-by-step facilitation script:**

1. **Frame (4 min).** Say: *"Think of energy like a simple ledger — entries in, entries out. This is not a mood diary and not a health record: you're logging* activities *and their effect on your energy, the way you'd track spending. The WHO's recommended actions for individuals include stress-management skills and leisure and physical activity — so the ledger deliberately asks about movement and genuine leisure, because those are the deposits most of us skip first when work gets loud."*
2. **Teach the ledger (4 min).** Walk through the columns on the handout. Say: *"Each evening, sixty seconds: two or three entries. A drain is anything that left you flatter — a 90-minute meeting with no break, an inbox marathon. A gain is anything that left you fuller — a real lunch, a walk, coffee with a friend, prayer or quiet time, sport, music. Mark gains that involved movement with an M and genuine leisure with an L. No ratings, no scores — just entries."*
3. **Set the contract (2 min).** Say: *"One week. Then, on your next Friday 15, read your ledger and make exactly one adjustment: one drain to shrink or one gain to repeat on purpose. One. Small and done beats grand and abandoned."*

**Debrief questions (for a follow-up session, volunteers only):**
- "What was your most reliable gain — and was it an M or an L?"
- "What drain surprised you by being on the list at all?"
- "What one adjustment did you choose?"

**Safety notes / what NOT to do:**
- The ledger logs activities and energy, never emotions, symptoms, sleep quality scores, or anything resembling a clinical diary. Do not add numeric scales.
- In debrief, never analyze anyone's ledger or draw conclusions about their state; ask, don't interpret.

**Low-tech variant:** Any notebook page with two columns, "− drains / + gains," dated per day.

**Participant handout — full text (Handout 4.7 — the printable ledger):**

> ### Energy Ledger — One Week
> *Sixty seconds each evening. Log activities, not feelings. This is a habit tool, not a health record — no scores, no ratings.*
> *Mark gains: **M** = involved physical movement, **L** = genuine leisure (something done purely for enjoyment).*
>
> | Day | − Drains (what flattened my energy) | + Gains (what restored it) | M/L |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Mon | | | |
> | Tue | | | |
> | Wed | | | |
> | Thu | | | |
> | Fri | | | |
> | Sat | | | |
> | Sun | | | |
>
> **End-of-week reading (do this during your Friday 15):**
> - My most reliable gain this week: ____________________
> - A drain I hadn't noticed before: ____________________
> - **My ONE adjustment for next week** (shrink one drain *or* deliberately repeat one gain): ____________________
>
> *Why M and L matter: leisure and physical activity are among the WHO-recommended ways individuals protect their wellbeing at work. They are deposits, not rewards — you don't have to earn them first.*

## 5. Manager-Session Add-On Exercises (W2 Only)

These two exercises are for the manager session defined in `WORKSHOP_SYSTEM_BLUEPRINT.md`. They train managers in *support and structure* — never in assessment. The WHO recommends manager training precisely so that managers can respond supportively and adjust conditions; nothing in W2 teaches managers to evaluate anyone's mental state.

### 5.1 M1 — The Supportive Conversation (S — Support)

**Facilitator card**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Objective | Managers rehearse a strictly bounded supportive conversation: listen, acknowledge, ask what support *at work* would help, offer workplace adjustments, signpost professional resources. They also learn what a manager must never do. |
| DESK letter | S — Support |
| Time | 35 minutes |
| Group size | Trios (speaker / listener / observer), rotating |
| Materials | Handout 5.1 (the script skeleton), scenario slips describing *work situations only* (e.g., "a team member says the workload has become impossible since the departure of a colleague") |

**Step-by-step facilitation script:**

1. **Frame (6 min).** Say: *"When a team member tells you they're struggling with work, you have one job, and it is not diagnosis. Your job is support and structure: listen, acknowledge, ask what would help at work, adjust what you can, and signpost professional resources for everything beyond work. You are a manager, not a clinician — and staying in your lane is not coldness, it's respect. The skeleton on your handout has five moves. In practice you will follow them in order, and there are two hard rules: never probe symptoms, never probe personal life."*
2. **Model (5 min).** Read the five moves aloud with the sample lines from Handout 5.1.
3. **Trio practice (18 min).** Two rotations of ~8 minutes: "team member" reads a scenario slip and improvises *work details only*; "manager" runs the skeleton; observer checks each move off and notes any probing. Say before starting: *"Team members: keep it about work — that's the scenario and the rule. Managers: if you catch yourself asking 'why' about anything personal, stop and return to move three."*
4. **Debrief (6 min).**

**Debrief questions:**
- "Which move felt least natural — and which will matter most in real life?"
- "Observers: where did the pull toward probing show up?"
- "What adjustment did you offer — and was it something you can actually deliver?"

**Safety notes / what NOT to do:**
- Scenario slips must contain only workplace situations. No scenarios involving disclosed conditions, crises, family circumstances, or grief.
- If managers ask "but what if someone discloses something serious?", the answer is: acknowledge with care, do not probe, keep it private, signpost the referral card, and follow the organization's own policy plus `RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md`. A manager's role in a serious disclosure is a warm handover, not an intervention.
- Ban the phrases "What's wrong with you?", "Are you depressed?", and any symptom question, explicitly, during the frame.

**Low-tech variant:** Write the five moves on the board; scenarios dictated.

**Participant handout — full text (Handout 5.1):**

> ### The Supportive Conversation — Script Skeleton for Managers
> *Your role: support and structure. Not diagnosis, not counseling, not investigation.*
>
> **Move 1 — LISTEN.** Let them finish. Don't fix yet.
> Sample line: "Thank you for telling me. Take your time — I'm listening."
>
> **Move 2 — ACKNOWLEDGE.** Reflect the *work situation*, without interpreting feelings.
> Sample line: "So since the handover, you're carrying two roles' worth of deadlines. That's a real load."
>
> **Move 3 — ASK WHAT WOULD HELP AT WORK.** This is the pivot question — memorize it.
> Sample line: **"What support at work would make a difference right now?"**
>
> **Move 4 — OFFER WORKPLACE ADJUSTMENTS.** Offer only what you can deliver: reprioritization, a deadline shift, redistribution, clearer scope, a regular check-in on workload.
> Sample line: "Here's what I can do this week: X moves to next sprint, and we'll review the load together every Monday."
>
> **Move 5 — SIGNPOST.** Normalize professional resources without implying they need them.
> Sample line: "And separately from work — the referral card from the wellbeing session lists where qualified support is available, for any of us, anytime."
>
> **The two hard rules:**
> 1. **Never probe symptoms.** No "are you anxious/depressed," no questions about sleep, mood, or health.
> 2. **Never probe personal life.** If they volunteer something personal: acknowledge kindly, keep it private, return to what you can adjust at work, signpost.
>
> *A supportive manager changes working conditions and opens doors. Qualified professionals do the rest.*

### 5.2 M2 — Team Workload Weather Report (D / E)

**Facilitator card**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Objective | Give managers a recurring 5-minute team ritual that surfaces workload pressure early — using weather language about the *work*, never questions about feelings. |
| DESK letters | D — Detect / E — Establish |
| Time | 15 minutes to teach |
| Group size | Manager cohort; then used in their own team meetings |
| Materials | Handout 5.2 |

**Step-by-step facilitation script:**

1. **Frame (4 min).** Say: *"Overload is easiest to fix early, and hardest to see from above. This ritual makes it visible in one round of a normal team meeting. The genius of weather language is that it describes the* workload*, not the person: 'my week is stormy' is information about conditions, not a disclosure about mental state. Nobody is asked how they feel — ever."*
2. **Teach the ritual (6 min).** Walk through Handout 5.2: the question, the scale, the two rules, the manager's follow-up.
3. **Practice one round (5 min).** Run a live round with the managers about *their own* current week, then model the follow-up: *"Two storms in the room — let's look at what can move."*

**Debrief questions:**
- "What will you do the first time someone reports 'storm' three weeks in a row?" (Answer to land: adjust conditions and, if appropriate, have a Supportive Conversation — moves 1–5.)
- "How will you keep this from becoming a performance ritual where everyone says 'sunny'?" (Answer to land: the manager reports honestly first, and storms visibly lead to help, never to blame.)

**Safety notes / what NOT to do:**
- The question is always about *workload*, never "how are you feeling?" Do not let teams drift the ritual into a mood check-in.
- Weather reports are never recorded in performance systems or used in evaluations — state this to teams explicitly.

**Low-tech variant:** Verbal round only; no artifacts needed.

**Participant handout — full text (Handout 5.2):**

> ### Team Workload Weather Report — a 5-minute team ritual
> **The question (same every week):** "One word on your *workload* this week — sunny, cloudy, or stormy?"
> - **Sunny** — load is manageable; normal week.
> - **Cloudy** — load is heavy; watch it; small adjustments welcome.
> - **Stormy** — load is over capacity; something needs to move *this week*.
>
> **The two rules:**
> 1. It's about the workload, never the person. Nobody asks "why," and nobody asks about feelings.
> 2. Storms trigger help, never blame. A storm report is a flare, and flares get answered.
>
> **The manager's follow-up:** every storm gets a same-week workload conversation using the Supportive Conversation skeleton — starting with: "What support at work would make a difference right now?"
>
> **The manager's honesty rule:** the manager reports first, truthfully. Teams copy what leaders model.

## 7. Printable Handout Masters

### 7.1 Take-home one-pager — "The DESK Method on One Page"

> # The DESK Method (proposed) — on one page
> *Practical skills for managing stress and workload at work. Educational, not clinical.*
>
> Almost 60% of the world's population is in work — and the WHO estimates 12 billion working days are lost every year to depression and anxiety. The workplace conditions that put wellbeing at risk are known: excessive workload and pace, long or inflexible hours, lack of control, under-used skills, and culture problems. The skills that protect you are learnable. That is what DESK is.
>
> **D — Detect.** Know your early overload signals — body, behavior, thinking. They are a fuel light, not a diagnosis. Read your dashboard early. *(Tool: Stress Signal Map.)*
> **E — Establish.** Make workload visible, then set structure around it: boundary sentences prepared in advance (acknowledge + limit + alternative), protected white space, communication hygiene. *(Tools: Workload X-Ray, Boundary Scripts, White-Space Audit.)*
> **S — Support.** Help-seeking is a skill. Three rings: self-management → workplace support (adjustments, not rescue) → professional help (the right address for persistent or heavy distress — using it is strength, and mental health is a basic human right). *(Tool: Help-Seeking Compass + referral card.)*
> **K — Keep.** Habits decay without maintenance. Fifteen minutes weekly: review load, flag risks, protect white space, name one ask. Log your energy drains and gains — and treat leisure and movement as deposits, not rewards. *(Tools: Friday 15, Energy Ledger.)*
>
> **The one-sentence version:** notice early, structure deliberately, ask skillfully, maintain weekly.
