Risk, Ethics & Referral Protocol

Dorina Salman — Mental Health Management Authority Plan (Lebanon)

Status: Binding operating document. Every piece of content, every workshop, every DM reply, and every partnership operates inside the boundaries defined here. If any other document in this package ever appears to conflict with this protocol, this protocol wins.

1. Purpose & Scope

1.1 Why this document exists

Dorina is building authority in mental health management — meaning practical self-management, workplace habits, stress literacy, boundaries, routines, help-seeking, and non-clinical educational workshops. She is not a licensed clinician, and nothing in this strategy positions her as one.

This is not a limitation to apologize for. It is the foundation of her credibility. The World Health Organization explicitly recommends worker mental health literacy and awareness training as an effective workplace intervention — delivered by educators and trained facilitators, not only clinicians. That is exactly the lane this plan occupies. Staying visibly, rigorously inside that lane is what will make employers, partners, and Lebanon's mental health ecosystem trust her.

1.2 What is in scope (Dorina may do)

  • Deliver educational workshops and content on: stress literacy, burnout prevention, workload and boundary habits, emotional organization, routines, workplace wellbeing culture, help-seeking awareness, and stigma reduction.
  • Share general, source-based information from authoritative bodies (WHO, Lebanon's Ministry of Public Health National Mental Health Programme) with citation.
  • Facilitate structured, non-clinical group exercises (reflection, planning, habit design, communication practice).
  • Signpost and refer people to qualified professionals and official services.
  • Speak from personal and professional experience, clearly labeled as such.

1.3 What is out of scope (Dorina must never do)

  • Diagnose, assess, screen, or label any person's mental condition — formally or informally, even casually ("that sounds like anxiety/ADHD/depression/trauma").
  • Provide therapy, counseling, psychological treatment, or any intervention presented as treatment.
  • Provide crisis intervention or act as a crisis service. Her role in a crisis is recognize → stabilize the moment → refer → hand over.
  • Give medical advice, comment on medication (starting, stopping, dosing, side effects), or recommend for/against any treatment.
  • Give legal advice or formal HR advice (disciplinary matters, terminations, accommodations rulings, labor disputes).
  • Run one-on-one "sessions" that resemble therapy in structure, privacy, or depth — even if labeled "coaching."
  • Interpret anyone's past trauma, relationships, or symptoms.

1.4 Who this protocol binds

Dorina, anyone assisting her (content support, moderation, workshop assistants), and any partner co-delivering an event under her name.

5. Workshop Safety Rules

These rules are part of the facilitator standard, announced at the opening of each session:

  1. Education, not therapy. The verbal disclaimer (4.3) opens every session.
  2. No forced disclosure. No exercise ever requires a participant to share personal emotional content. Every sharing prompt includes a pass option: "share only what feels comfortable, or pass — passing is always fine."
  3. Work-level, not wound-level. Exercises target habits, workload, routines, and communication — never childhood, trauma, grief, relationships, or clinical symptoms. If a participant takes an exercise there, gently redirect (see Section 10).
  4. Group agreements at the start: confidentiality ("what's shared here stays here"), no advice-giving between participants on personal matters, respect for silence and passing, phones away or on silent.
  5. The exit is always open. Participants may leave any exercise or the room without explanation. State this explicitly.
  6. No singling out. Never ask a specific person to share how they "really feel." Prompts go to the room, volunteers respond.
  7. Referral information visible. Every session ends with a "Where to find real support in Lebanon" slide/handout (Section 7). This is non-negotiable, even for corporate audiences.
  8. Two-adult standard for follow-up. Any post-workshop conversation that turns personal happens in a semi-public space (open room, corner of the venue), not behind closed doors, and is kept short and referral-oriented.
  9. No recordings of participant sharing. Sessions may record the facilitator segments only. Group discussion is never recorded (see Section 9).
  10. Debrief every session. After each workshop, Dorina notes (without names): did any red flags occur, was a referral given, did any exercise need to be stopped. This log improves the protocol.

6. Participant Red Flags

Dorina and any assistant must know these signals. A red flag does not mean panic — it means shift from facilitator mode to referral mode.

6.1 Immediate-action red flags (respond now, session pauses if needed)

  • Any mention of wanting to die, ending their life, self-harm, or "not wanting to be here anymore" — even said jokingly.
  • Statements suggesting intent to harm someone else.
  • A participant appears disoriented, dissociated (unresponsive, "not there"), or has a panic episode that is not settling.
  • Disclosure of ongoing abuse or violence (domestic, workplace, or otherwise).

Response pattern: stay calm → move the person gently out of the group's focus (offer to step aside with them and an assistant/organizer) → do not probe for details → use the crisis referral wording (Section 7) → hand over to the workplace contact person or accompany them to call the helpline → never leave a person who mentioned suicidal thoughts alone before a handover happens → document factually (Section 9.4).

6.2 Elevated-concern red flags (do not interrupt the session; act at the next break or end)

  • Crying that does not settle after a normal pause, or visible distress disproportionate to the exercise.
  • Disclosures of a diagnosed condition with questions about treatment or medication ("should I stop my medication?" → this is always and only referred out).
  • Repeated self-critical or hopeless language ("nothing will ever change," "I'm broken").
  • A participant seeking one-on-one "sessions" or repeated private emotional conversations with Dorina.
  • Signs of substance-related impairment during a session.

Response pattern: acknowledge without analyzing ("I hear that this is heavy — thank you for trusting the room") → redirect the exercise to the group → at the break, check in briefly and privately, offer the referral handout, and if relevant use the soft referral script (7.2).

6.3 What is not a red flag

Normal emotion is expected in wellbeing workshops: brief tears, sighs, frustration about workload, "this job is burning me out." These get empathy and normal facilitation, not a referral escalation. Over-escalating is its own harm — it stigmatizes normal feelings.

7.3 Crisis script — when someone mentions suicide, self-harm, or harm to others

English:

"I'm really glad you told me. I want you to know I take what you said seriously, and you deserve real support from people trained for exactly this. I'm not able to provide that support myself, but I will stay with you right now while we contact someone who can. Embrace Lebanon publicly lists 1564 for its Lifeline; if this is still current and available, can we call together now? If not, we will contact the verified local emergency/support service."

Arabic:

"أنا فعلًا مبسوطة إنك حكيتلي. بدي تعرف/تعرفي إني آخذ كلامك على محمل الجد، وإنت تستاهل/تستاهلي دعم حقيقي من ناس متدرّبين تمامًا لهالشي. أنا ما فيني قدّم هالدعم بنفسي، بس رح ضل معك هلق لنتواصل مع حدا بيقدر. الخط الساخن هو ١٥٦٤ — مجاني وسرّي. فينا نتصل سوا؟"

Rules for crisis moments: - Never promise secrecy ("I won't tell anyone") — say instead: "I'll only share what's needed to keep you safe." - Never probe for method, plan, or history. That is a clinician's conversation. - If the person refuses help and there is immediate danger, involve the venue/employer contact person and the currently verified local emergency service. Safety overrides confidentiality. Verify the correct emergency number before each workshop. - Follow up is a handover, not a treatment relationship: one check-in message referring back to the resources is appropriate; ongoing emotional support conversations are not.

8. DM & Comment Response Scripts

As Dorina's audience grows, people will message her with personal struggles. Every response follows one shape: warmth → honesty about role → referral → boundary.

8.1 DM: someone shares personal struggles (non-crisis)

"Thank you for trusting me with this — it takes courage to write it. I want to be straight with you: I'm an educator, not a therapist, so it wouldn't be right for me to advise you on something this personal. What I can tell you is that talking to a qualified professional genuinely helps, and in Lebanon the Lifeline 1564 is free and confidential, and the Ministry of Public Health's National Mental Health Programme lists real services. My content will always be here for the skills side — but for what you're carrying, you deserve proper support. 🤍"

8.2 DM: crisis language (mentions of suicide or self-harm)

"I'm very glad you reached out, and I'm taking what you wrote seriously. Please contact the Lifeline right now at 1564 — it's free, confidential, and they are trained for exactly this. If you are in immediate danger, contact the currently verified local emergency service immediately. I'm an educator, not a crisis professional, so I can't give you the support you deserve here — but the people at 1564 can, today. Please message them now."

Then: do not continue a back-and-forth emotional conversation. If they reply, restate the referral once, warmly. Never go silent without the referral; never become the support line.

8.3 DM: asking for a private "session"

"Thank you for asking — I'm honored. To be transparent: I don't offer one-on-one sessions, because personal support is clinical work and I'm an educator, not a therapist. What I do offer is group workshops on stress skills and workplace habits — and if that's useful for you or your team, I'd love to have you. For personal support, I always recommend a licensed professional; the National Mental Health Programme (moph.gov.lb) and Lifeline 1564 are the right doors."

8.4 DM: asking about medication or diagnosis

"That's an important question — and exactly the kind that deserves a qualified answer, which I can't responsibly give. Anything about diagnosis or medication belongs with a doctor or licensed mental health professional. Please don't change anything based on posts or messages — including mine. 🤍"

8.5 Public comment: someone discloses distress under a post

"Thank you for sharing this here — I know that's not easy. 🤍 I've sent you a private message with support options. For anyone reading who's struggling: the Lifeline 1564 (Lebanon) is free and confidential."

Then send 8.1 or 8.2 privately. Never discuss their situation in public comments.

8.6 Public comment: hostile / "you're not even qualified"

"You're right that I'm not a clinician — and I say so in every workshop and post. My work is educational: skills, habits, and awareness, based on WHO and Ministry of Public Health resources. Anything clinical, I refer to licensed professionals. Thanks for giving me the chance to make that clear."

One reply maximum. No arguments in comments. Repeat offenders are ignored, not blocked, unless abusive.

8.7 Response-time boundary

Set a visible norm (e.g., in bio or auto-reply): messages are answered within 1–2 business days. This prevents the audience from treating DMs as a live support channel. Crisis DMs are the only exception: answer with 8.2 as soon as seen, whatever the hour.

9. Privacy Rules

  1. Participant stories are never content. No workshop moment, quote, or anecdote appears in any post — even anonymized — without written consent from the person involved. "A participant once told me…" posts require consent or must be fully fictionalized and labeled as illustrative.
  2. Photos and recordings: photos at workshops require announced consent and an opt-out (a no-photo zone or sticker). Group discussions are never recorded. Attendee lists are never published.
  3. DMs are confidential. Screenshots of private messages are never posted, even praised, even anonymized, without explicit written consent.
  4. Red-flag documentation is minimal and factual: date, context, what was observed (behavior, not interpretation — "participant mentioned self-harm," not "participant seemed suicidal/depressed"), action taken, who it was handed to. No diagnosis language ever. Stored privately (password-protected, not in shared drives), retained for accountability, never shared with the employer beyond what safety required in the moment.
  5. Employer reporting: post-workshop reports to a client contain themes and attendance only — never individual names linked to what they said, asked, or disclosed. If an employer asks "who seemed to be struggling," the answer is a polite refusal plus an offer to share how the company can provide support channels for everyone.
  6. Contact data (registration emails/phones) is used only for the stated purpose (logistics, follow-up materials) and never sold, shared, or moved into marketing lists without explicit opt-in.
  7. Testimonials require written consent, show only what the person approved (name/initials/role level), and never reference personal mental health details.

10. When to Stop a Workshop Exercise

An exercise stops — smoothly, without drama — when any of the following occurs:

  1. A participant shows immediate-action red flags (6.1).
  2. Multiple participants become visibly distressed and the room's emotional temperature keeps rising rather than settling.
  3. An exercise drifts from work-level to wound-level: participants begin disclosing trauma, grief, family crises, or clinical symptoms.
  4. Participants begin diagnosing or "fixing" each other ("you clearly have anxiety, my cousin took…").
  5. A conflict between participants turns personal.
  6. Dorina herself feels out of her depth. This instinct is always honored, never overridden by schedule pressure.

The graceful stop (script):

"I'm going to pause us here — thank you all for how seriously you took this. Some of what's coming up is bigger than an exercise, and it deserves more than an exercise. Let's take a five-minute breather, and when we come back we'll move to [next activity]. And a reminder that the resource sheet at the end has real support options — I'd genuinely encourage using them."

Rules for the stop: - Never name the person or moment that triggered the stop. - Always pivot to something (a break, a lighter structured activity), never just end abruptly. - Check in privately with any visibly affected participant at the break (Section 6 response patterns). - Log it in the post-session debrief (Rule 10, Section 5) and adjust or retire the exercise if it repeatedly runs hot.

Design rule upstream of all of this: exercises should be built so this section is rarely needed — prompts about workload, schedules, habits, and communication, not feelings-excavation. Prevention beats intervention.

12. Employer & Workplace Boundaries

Corporate workshops are the core commercial channel, and they carry their own ethical lines:

  1. Contracted scope is educational. Every proposal and invoice describes deliverables as educational workshops/training on workplace wellbeing and stress-management skills — never "employee mental health support," "counseling," or "psychological services."
  2. No individual reporting. Dorina never reports to management on individuals — who attended reluctantly, who cried, who asked what. See Privacy Rule 5. This is stated up front in the proposal so it never becomes a negotiation.
  3. Not an HR instrument. Dorina's workshops are not: a substitute for an employee assistance program, a performance-management tool, evidence-gathering before terminations, or a box-ticking response to a specific internal incident (harassment case, suicide of an employee, layoffs) — unless the engagement is redesigned with appropriate professionals involved and her role clearly limited to the educational component.
  4. No mediation or investigations. Interpersonal conflicts, grievances, and disputes are HR/legal territory. If they surface in a session, they are acknowledged and redirected to the company's own channels.
  5. The employer gets recommendations, not gossip. Post-engagement reports may include anonymized themes ("workload and unclear priorities were recurring topics") plus WHO-aligned organizational recommendations (manager training, workload review, flexibility) — this positions Dorina as strategic while protecting participants.
  6. Attendance should be voluntary or at minimum non-punitive. Dorina requests that employers frame attendance as offered, not enforced, and never tie attendance to evaluation.
  7. A named employer contact (HR or organizer) is agreed before every corporate session as the handover point for red-flag situations. Dorina does not carry safety follow-up alone inside someone else's organization.
  8. Conflict-of-interest hygiene: while employed at TiKiT (or any employer), Dorina's own workplace is not used as content material without explicit employer consent, colleagues are not case studies, and any workshop delivered to her own employer follows all the same rules — plus extra care that colleagues never confuse her educational role with a confidential support role.
  9. Right to decline: engagements that pressure her toward clinical territory ("can you also do one-on-ones with the struggling employees?") are declined or reshaped, with a referral to qualified providers. The polite line: "That part deserves a licensed professional — I can recommend the right doors, and I'll gladly handle the education side."
