Workshop System Blueprint — Dorina Salman

Purpose: This document is the complete operating system for Dorina's workshop business — the philosophy and safety frame, the full product ladder (W0–W5), minute-by-minute run sheets for the three workhorse formats, the facilitator guide, logistics and operations, the participant journey from invitation to testimonial, the closure system, and the measurement framework. Exercise scripts and printable materials live in WORKSHOP_EXERCISES_AND_HANDOUTS.md; how workshops are sold lives in MARKETING_FUNNEL_AND_CAMPAIGNS.md; where to sell them lives in OPPORTUNITY_MAP_LEBANON.md.

Educational content on workplace wellbeing — not therapy, diagnosis, or crisis support. If you're struggling, please reach a qualified professional; in an emergency, contact local emergency services.

1. Workshop Philosophy & Safety Frame

1.1 Education, not therapy — the line that protects everyone

Every workshop Dorina delivers is a class, not a clinic. She teaches skills — stress literacy, boundaries, workload structure, help-seeking as a competence — the way a first-aid trainer teaches bandaging: practical, universal, and explicitly not a substitute for professional care. This position is not a limitation; it is the product. WHO's guidance on mental health at work names worker mental health literacy and awareness training as a recommended workplace intervention precisely because it improves knowledge and reduces stigma — and that is exactly the lane Dorina occupies.

Three sentences define the frame, and Dorina says versions of them out loud in every session:

  1. "I am an educator, not a therapist." Nothing in the room is diagnosis, treatment, or assessment.
  2. "We work on situations, not conditions." Participants discuss workload, routines, and habits — never symptoms, disorders, or medical histories.
  3. "Part of this skill is knowing when to hand over." Help-seeking and referral to qualified professionals are taught as strengths, and the referral signpost appears in every session (see Section 7 and RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md).

In Arabic, the frame lands naturally as: "أنا هون لعلّم مهارات، مش لعالج" ("I'm here to teach skills, not to treat") — warm, colloquial, and unambiguous.

1.2 The DESK Method (proposed) as session architecture

The DESK Method (proposed) is not only the content — it is the structure of every session. Whatever the format or length, the session moves through the same arc:

DESK stage In-session function Typical share of time
D — Detect Build stress literacy; participants notice and name their own signals without stigma ~25–30%
E — Establish Convert awareness into structure: boundaries, routines, workload visibility ~30–35%
S — Support Reframe help-seeking as a skill; clarify what workplace support is and is not; signpost referral pathways ~15–20%
K — Keep Commit to sustaining habits: weekly reviews, rest as a system, one concrete next step ~15–20%

This predictable arc means participants always leave with (a) something noticed, (b) something structured, (c) somewhere to turn, and (d) something to keep doing. It also means Dorina never improvises into risky territory: if a moment does not fit D, E, S, or K, it does not belong in the room.

1.3 The "container" concept

Lebanese corporate rooms mix hierarchy, humor, and healthy skepticism. The workshop works because Dorina builds a container — a predictable, low-pressure space — announced in the first five minutes of every session:

  • Predictability. The agenda is visible from minute one. Participants always know what comes next and when the session ends. No surprises, no ambush exercises.
  • Opt-out rights. Every exercise is invitation, not obligation. The standing line: "Everything here is optional. Pass at any time — no explanation needed." In Arabic: "كل شي هون اختياري" ("everything here is optional").
  • No forced sharing. Personal reflection stays on paper unless a participant chooses to speak. Pair and group discussions are framed around work situations, not private lives. Dorina never cold-calls a person to share something personal; she cold-calls only for opinions on the material.
  • Confidentiality norm. "What's said here about personal experience stays here; what's learned here leaves with you." This is stated, not assumed — especially important when managers and their direct reports are in the same room.
  • The exit ramp. Anyone may step out at any time without comment. Dorina normalizes this in the opening ("the door is not locked, and the coffee is not rationed") so leaving the room is never conspicuous.

The container is also Dorina's professional protection: a room where sharing is optional and topics are situational is a room that stays educational.

  • Audience: Mixed staff at any SME, agency, law firm, or nonprofit/humanitarian organization — the door-opener for decision-makers to see Dorina work.
  • Group size: 8–30. Format: In-person or online; deliberately low-tech (works with a flipchart if the projector fails).

W1 — Mental Health Management at Work («إدارة الصحة النفسية في العمل» — managing mental health at work)

  • Duration: Half day, 3.5–4 hours including breaks. The flagship.
  • Audience: Full teams (mixed levels) in SMEs, agencies, law firms, startups; nonprofit/humanitarian organization staff teams.
  • Group size: 10–20 (above 20, interaction quality drops; propose two cohorts instead).
  • Format: In-person strongly preferred (the exercises are tactile); hybrid possible with an in-room co-host handling the remote side.

  • DESK coverage: Full method, all four stages, with take-home tools for each.
  • Price: Suggested $500–$1,200 per session depending on organization size and customization (to validate).

W2 — The Supportive Manager («المدير الداعم» — the supportive manager)

  • Duration: 2 hours. Audience: People managers and team leads; HR business partners.
  • Group size: 6–15 managers. Format: In-person or online.
  • Rationale: WHO explicitly recommends manager training as a core workplace mental health action, and names workplace risks managers directly control — excessive workloads and work pace, understaffing, long or inflexible hours, lack of control over job design, and under-use of skills. W2 is Dorina's non-clinical translation of that recommendation.
  • Learning outcomes: Managers can (1) spot team-level overload signals (missed deadlines patterns, withdrawal, uncharacteristic irritability — observed behavior, never inferred diagnosis), (2) redesign workload distribution using a simple visibility tool, (3) hold a supportive, strictly non-clinical one-to-one conversation using a listen–acknowledge–ask–signpost structure, (4) refer onward correctly: to HR, to EAP-type resources where they exist, or to qualified professional pathways — never playing counselor themselves.
  • DESK coverage: D and E at team level; S in depth (the manager as signpost, not support-provider of last resort); K as management routine.
  • Price: Suggested $300–$700 per session (to validate). Often sold as an add-on to W1 ("train the team, equip the managers").

W3 — The Organized Calm («الهدوء المنظّم» — organized calm), for administrative professionals & EAs

  • Duration: 2.5–3 hours. Audience: Dorina's tribe — administrative professionals, executive assistants, office managers; the people who absorb everyone else's urgency.
  • Group size: 8–20. Format: In-person or online; also suitable as a public (open-enrollment) session where individuals pay their own seat.
  • Why it exists: This is where Dorina's 12+ years of lived credibility is strongest. Admin professionals sit at the intersection of everyone's deadlines with the least formal authority to push back — the exact "lack of control" risk WHO identifies. Nobody teaches this room better than someone who lived in it.
  • Learning outcomes: Participants can (1) detect the specific overload pattern of support roles (invisible workload, interrupt-driven days), (2) make invisible work visible to their managers with a one-page tool, (3) deploy boundary scripts that respect hierarchy while protecting focus, (4) build a personal weekly reset that survives a chaotic office.
  • DESK coverage: Full method through the lens of support roles; E is the deepest module.
  • Price: Suggested $250–$600 per corporate session; for open-enrollment, suggested $25–$50 per individual seat (both to validate).

W4 — DESK Online («DESK أونلاين»)

  • Duration: 90 minutes live online. Audience: Remote/distributed teams; diaspora and GCC-based Lebanese professionals; organizations avoiding travel or venue costs.
  • Group size: 8–25. Format: Online, camera-on encouraged but never required (bandwidth realities in Lebanon make camera-optional the respectful default).
  • Learning outcomes: A compressed W1: personal stress-signal mapping, one workload-visibility tool, one boundary script practiced in breakout pairs, help-seeking signposting, and the weekly review as the "keep" commitment.
  • DESK coverage: All four stages, one tool each.

W5 — The DESK Quarterly (refresher retainer)

  • Duration: 60–90 minutes per quarter, four sessions per year. Audience: Organizations that have completed W1 (prerequisite).
  • Format: Hybrid by default — alternate in-person and online across the year to manage cost and logistics.
  • Structure: Each quarter revisits one DESK stage in depth with the team's own data: Q1 Detect (re-map signals; what changed?), Q2 Establish (boundary and workload tune-up), Q3 Support (help-seeking culture check), Q4 Keep (annual reset and rest planning — pairs naturally with the December/January arc in 12_MONTH_LINKEDIN_CONTENT_CALENDAR.md).

4. Facilitator Guide

4.1 Preparation checklist (run T-7 days, T-1 day, T-0)

T-7 days: - [ ] Intake questionnaire (§5.2) returned and reviewed; customization notes written (sector language, team structure, known sensitivities). - [ ] Participant count confirmed; materials kit (§5.3) packed with 15% surplus handouts. - [ ] Room details confirmed: layout (cabaret/U-shape preferred; classroom rows discouraged), projector, flipchart, power situation, generator schedule if relevant. - [ ] Slides finalized; PDF backup exported to phone and USB. - [ ] Client reminded to send the participant invitation (§6.1) if not yet sent.

T-1 day: - [ ] Print run: handouts, commitment cards, referral cards, feedback forms, name tents. - [ ] Referral card contents re-verified against RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md — including the line "[TO VERIFY before use: current national emotional-support helpline number — check the Ministry of Public Health / National Mental Health Programme site]" replaced with the verified current number. - [ ] Confirm start time and arrival logistics with the client contact; confirm who opens the room. - [ ] Personal preparation: rehearse the opening five minutes aloud; sleep is part of the job.

T-0 (arrive 45 minutes early): - [ ] Room set: seating, materials at seats, flipchart tested, slide 1 up, music optional and low. - [ ] Identify the physical "bridge space" for any break-time 1:1 (a corner or side room within sight of others — never fully isolated). - [ ] Brief the client contact: agenda, break times, and "if anyone leaves the room, no fuss."

4.2 Room & energy management

  • Open with warmth, not authority. Lebanese rooms give respect to competence shown, not claimed. Dorina's credential is her lived admin experience plus the quality of the material — she never needs to inflate it, and must never imply clinical credentials.
  • Read the energy every 20 minutes. Post-lunch dip: switch to pair work. Restless energy: stand-up exercise. Heavy silence after a discussion: name it lightly ("this one landed — take thirty seconds, then talk to your neighbor").
  • Physical movement is built in — the run sheets alternate solo/pair/plenary deliberately. Never lecture longer than 15 minutes without an interaction.
  • Time is a promise. Ending on time is part of the container. If a segment runs long, cut content, not the break and never the closure sequence.
  • Pre-planned flex cuts. Each run sheet has designated "accordion" segments so cutting is a decision made in advance, not under pressure: in W0, the pair rehearsal compresses to a demo; in W1, D2 (naming discussion) can shrink to 8 minutes and E3 (White-Space Audit) can move to homework with a one-minute setup; in W2, the workload-design discussion compresses before the conversation practice ever does — the practice segment is the session's spine.
  • Hierarchy in the room. If a senior leader attends, greet them normally and give them no special role; if they dominate, the standard redirect applies (Dorina's calm evenhandedness with seniority is itself a demonstration of boundaries — participants notice). If leadership presence visibly freezes the room, switch more work to paper and pairs, where safety returns.

4.3 Bilingual delivery (Arabic/English code-switching)

Lebanese corporate rooms code-switch constantly; fighting it loses the room. Working norms:

  • Slides and handouts in English (the default written register of Lebanese business), with key terms glossed in Arabic on the slide where the term carries emotional weight — e.g., burnout (الاحتراق الوظيفي), boundaries (الحدود), stress signals (إشارات التوتر).
  • Spoken delivery mirrors the room. Open in whichever language the client contact used in emails, then match the participants. Stories and humor land best in Lebanese Arabic; frameworks and instructions are clearest in English. Dorina should switch deliberately, not apologetically.
  • Emotional-register rule: stigma-sensitive moments (D2 naming segment, S help-seeking segment) benefit from Arabic — it removes the distancing effect English can create around personal topics. Technical tools (Workload X-Ray, weekly review) stay anchored in English terms so participants can reuse them in written work.
  • Never translate live word-for-word — say it once, well, in the language that fits; repeat only headline instructions bilingually ("خمس دقايق — five minutes, then we regroup").
  • Ask in the intake questionnaire (§5.2) about language mix; nonprofit/humanitarian organization rooms may skew Arabic, agency rooms may skew English.

4.4 Handling difficult moments

Situation What Dorina does What she never does
Dominant participant Thank + redirect: "Great point — let's hear a different desk." Assign them a role (timekeeper, scribe) at the next exercise. If persistent, a friendly private word at the break. Publicly shut them down; let them convert the session into their platform.
Silence Wait a full 7 seconds (count silently — silence is thinking). Downgrade the ask: plenary question → pairs → written. Offer her own example first. Fill every silence herself; cold-call someone for personal content.
Skepticism ("this is soft stuff") Welcome it: "Healthy skepticism is a Detect signal working well." Answer with the operational frame — workload, hours, control are business design questions; WHO's working-days figure if numbers are wanted. Invite the skeptic to stress-test one tool. Argue, plead, or over-claim outcomes; take it personally.
The room turns into a complaints session about management Acknowledge once, then structure: "Real — and today's question is what's in your control; the manager version of this workshop exists too." (Genuinely note themes for the client debrief, anonymized.) Join the complaining; promise to "tell management"; let it consume an exercise.
Someone cries or is visibly moved Normalize quietly ("take your time — this stuff is real"), offer the exit ramp without spotlight, continue the session's flow so the person isn't the center of attention, and apply §4.5 at the break. Stop the whole room to process one person's emotion; probe for the cause.

4.5 Disclosure protocol summary (the critical page)

Full protocol: RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md. The in-session summary Dorina memorizes:

If a participant shares personal distress, mentions a mental health condition, or shows a possible crisis sign (in the room, in an exercise debrief, or on a worksheet):

  1. Acknowledge, briefly and warmly. "Thank you for trusting us with that — that sounds genuinely hard." Dignity first, always.
  2. Don't explore. No follow-up questions about the experience, history, symptoms, or causes. Not in plenary, not in pairs, not "just out of care." Exploration is clinical territory.
  3. Bridge to break-time 1:1. "I'd like to check in with you at the break — is that okay?" At the break, in the semi-visible bridge space: listen briefly, do not advise on the condition or situation, express care.
  4. Hand the referral card. The card (contents defined in RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md) lists qualified pathway types and the verified helpline line. Frame: "The strongest move in the whole DESK method is reaching the right person — this card is the map."
  5. Follow the protocol for anything beyond. Crisis signs, safety concerns, or requests for ongoing personal help all route through RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md — including when to inform the client contact and how to protect confidentiality while doing so.
  6. Never diagnose, assess, or advise on conditions. Not tentatively, not "off the record," not when directly asked ("do you think I have burnout?" → "I can't tell you that — and someone qualified genuinely can. Let me show you the pathway.").

This protocol is rehearsed, not improvised. Dorina practices the bridge sentence aloud before every session until it is automatic.

Track every engagement in a simple spreadsheet (pipeline stage, dates, format, feedback scores, rebook status) — this feeds Section 8 directly. The workflow tooling that supports this is described in CONTENT_APPROVAL_AND_AUTOMATION_WORKFLOW.md.

5.2 Client intake questionnaire (the actual questions)

Sent as a short form or document after agreement; answers drive customization.

  1. What does your organization do, and what does a typical week look like for the team attending? (Sector, team size, rhythm of the work.)
  2. Who exactly will attend — roles, seniority mix, and will managers and their direct reports be in the same room?
  3. How many participants do you expect? (We recommend 10–20 for interactive formats.)
  4. What prompted this workshop now? Was there a specific situation, season, or leadership decision behind it? (No names needed — context only.)
  5. What would make this a clear success for you three months from now?
  6. What language mix does the team naturally use in meetings — mostly Arabic, mostly English, or fluid switching?
  7. Are there topics or recent events we should handle with extra care (e.g., recent layoffs, restructuring, a loss in the team)? We will not raise them; we simply avoid stepping on them blind.
  8. What support does your organization currently offer employees (flexible hours, HR open door, any employee-assistance-type service, health coverage)? We reference only what actually exists.
  9. Room and logistics: What space is available, what is the seating, is there a projector and flipchart, and what is the electricity/generator situation during our time slot?
  10. For online/hybrid sessions: which platform does your team already use, and how reliable is participants' connectivity?
  11. Who is our on-the-day contact, and who should receive the post-workshop debrief summary?
  12. Anything else you want us to know before we design your session?

Scope note appended to the form (verbatim): "This workshop is educational — it teaches practical workplace wellbeing skills. It is not therapy, diagnosis, assessment, or crisis support, and it does not replace professional care. Our facilitator follows a strict referral protocol for any personal disclosures."

5.3 Materials kit list

Core kit (every session): printed handouts per run sheet (+15% surplus); commitment cards; referral cards (verified per §4.1); feedback forms + a QR-code version; name tents + markers; flipchart pads ×2 and working markers (test them — the eternal facilitator trap); masking tape; timer (phone in airplane mode); printed run sheet + facilitator notes; disclaimer/agenda slides as printed A4 backups; pens ×30; a small box for collected feedback forms.

Tech layer: laptop + charger; HDMI and USB-C adapters; USB stick with slides (PDF + editable); slides also on phone; portable speaker (optional); for W4/hybrid: headset, lighting, and the pre-sent PDF deck.

Low-tech fallback (power cuts are a planning assumption, not a surprise): the entire session must be deliverable with zero electricity — flipchart versions of every key slide pre-drawn on the pad's first pages; all exercises are paper-based by design; daylight-viable room requested in intake Q9. If power dies mid-session, the line is: "Perfect — the DESK Method was designed for real Lebanese conditions. Flipchart it is." (This moment, handled with humor, routinely becomes the most-remembered part of the day.)

5.4 Co-facilitation rule: what needs a licensed professional, what gets declined

Dorina delivers alone anything within the educational frame: stress literacy, boundaries, workload structure, routines, help-seeking as a skill, manager conversation structure.

Requires a licensed mental health professional as co-facilitator (Dorina may organize and co-host, never solo-deliver): - Any session marketed around a condition (anxiety, depression, trauma, grief) rather than workplace skills. - Psychological first aid training or anything derived from clinical protocols. - Sessions for teams in the immediate aftermath of a critical incident (death of a colleague, serious accident, security event). - Any format involving screening, assessment, or individual evaluation of participants.

Declined and referred out entirely (with a warm referral to qualified providers): - Requests for individual counseling, "coaching" of a struggling named employee, or mediation of an acute interpersonal/mental-health situation. - Crisis response or debriefing requests. - Anything a client insists on framing as treatment or therapy, even after scope clarification.

Declining well builds authority: "That's outside what an educator should ethically do — here is the kind of professional you need, and here is what I can do around it once the acute phase has passed." The referral-partner shortlist logic lives in RISK_ETHICS_AND_REFERRAL_PROTOCOL.md and OPPORTUNITY_MAP_LEBANON.md.

6. Participant Journey

6.1 Before — invitation copy snippet (client forwards to participants)

Subject: [Date] — a working session on managing pressure well (not a lecture)

Hi team — on [date] we're hosting [workshop name] with workplace wellbeing educator Dorina Salman. It's a practical, hands-on session: you'll map your own stress signals, get ready-to-use scripts for protecting your focus, and leave with a 15-minute weekly habit that keeps it all alive.

Three things to know going in: everything is optional — nobody is ever required to share anything personal; this is education, not therapy — we work on workload and habits, not private matters; and it ends on time. Bring a pen and your real calendar. See you there.

Expectation-setting is part of safety: participants who arrive knowing the container exists relax into it faster.

6.2 During

The run sheets (§3) and closure system (§7) define the in-session journey. Two participant-experience rules sit above everything: every participant leaves with paper in hand (their own completed worksheets — the workshop's tangible residue), and every participant hears the referral signpost twice (S segment + closure) without it ever feeling like an alarm.

6.3 After — the follow-up system

T+24h — Thank-you email (Dorina sends to the client contact for forwarding, or directly where emails were collected with consent):

Subject: Your DESK toolkit — and one small ask

Hi everyone — thank you for the energy you brought yesterday. A few things, briefly:

Your tools: attached is the PDF pack of everything we used — the Stress Signal Map, the Workload X-Ray, the boundary scripts, and The Friday 15 Weekly Review template. Print the weekly review; it works better on paper.

Your commitment: you each wrote one habit on your commitment card. That card is the whole workshop in one sentence — keep it where you'll see it on Friday.

The signpost, one more time: what we did together was education, not therapy. If anything in your life needs more than a habit — that's normal, that's human, and reaching a qualified professional is the strongest move in the entire method. The referral card from your pack has the pathways.

It was a privilege to spend the time with you. — Dorina

Educational content on workplace wellbeing — not therapy, diagnosis, or crisis support. If you're struggling, please reach a qualified professional; in an emergency, contact local emergency services.

T+7 days — one-habit nudge (short email or client-channel message):

Subject: Friday is coming — 15 minutes

Quick nudge from your workshop: this Friday, run The Friday 15 — 15 minutes, four questions: What signals did I notice this week? Which boundary held, which slipped? Do I need to ask for anything? What's my rest plan for the weekend? That's it. One week of doing it beats a hundred slides about it.

T+30 days — check-in + feedback survey. A short warm note ("one month on — two minutes of your honesty helps me make this better for the next team") linking to the survey:

  1. One month later, which single tool or idea from the workshop have you actually used? (Open text)
  2. On a scale of 1–5, how much do you agree: "I notice my own overload signals earlier than I did before the workshop."
  3. On a scale of 1–5: "I have used at least one boundary or workload tool from the session in real work."
  4. Have you run The Friday 15 Weekly Review at least once? (Yes, more than once / Yes, once / Not yet)
  5. What has gotten in the way of using what you learned? (Open text)
  6. On a scale of 0–10, how likely are you to recommend this workshop to a colleague or another team?
  7. What should be added, cut, or changed? (Open text)
  8. Optional: If any part of this experience helped you in a way you'd be willing to have quoted (anonymously or with your name — your choice, and you can withdraw it anytime), tell us here. Leaving this blank is completely fine.

6.4 From outcomes to testimonials and case summaries

  • Consent is explicit, written, and revocable. Only survey Q8 responses (or direct written offers) become testimonials, and only after Dorina confirms in writing how the person wants to be credited. No invented or "reconstructed" quotes, ever.
  • Case summaries are patterns, not people. After each engagement, Dorina writes a five-line internal summary: sector type, format, headline feedback scores, one anonymized theme, one iteration note. With client permission, an anonymized version ("a 15-person team at a Beirut services firm") can appear in proposals and in LinkedIn content per LINKEDIN_AUTHORITY_ENGINE.md.
  • Nothing disclosed in a session is ever content. Personal moments from the room — even anonymized, even moving — do not appear in marketing. Workshop design stories are shareable; participant lives are not.

6.5 The client debrief (T+7 to T+14) — the meeting that earns the rebooking

A 30-minute call or short written summary to the client contact, with a fixed agenda:

  1. What we did: format delivered, attendance, and the session's arc in three sentences.
  2. How it landed: headline feedback scores (value, safety, recommendation) against the quality bar in Section 8 — shared transparently, including anything below bar and what Dorina will change.
  3. What the room told us (anonymized): two or three workload/culture themes — never individuals, never disclosures. Where relevant, map themes to the WHO workplace risk categories (workload and pace, hours and flexibility, control, skills use, culture) so the client sees their situation in a recognized framework rather than as criticism.
  4. What the organization could do next: one or two low-cost structural suggestions within the client's control (e.g., a workload-visibility ritual, protected focus blocks) — offered as options, not audit findings.
  5. The natural next rung: the single most logical follow-on (W2 after W1; W5 after two engagements; the 1-month survey as the checkpoint) — proposed once, without pressure.

The debrief is where a workshop becomes a relationship. It is also the ethical firewall in action: the client receives patterns and scores, never names or stories.

7. Closure System — How Every Workshop Ends

The last ten minutes are identical in shape across W0–W5. Predictable endings are part of the container, and this sequence is where safety, habit, and business quietly converge.

  1. The commitment card. Each participant writes one specific habit ("I will…") on a physical card, keeps it, and — if willing — photographs it as their own reminder. Voluntary shares only, two or three, time-boxed. The card template is in WORKSHOP_EXERCISES_AND_HANDOUTS.md.
  2. The referral signposting slide. Always the penultimate slide, always delivered in the same calm tone: the levels of support, the reminder that professional care is the right tool for some things, the referral card in their pack, and the verified helpline line — "[TO VERIFY before use: current national emotional-support helpline number — check the Ministry of Public Health / National Mental Health Programme site]" replaced before first use. Never rushed, never skipped, never dramatic.
  3. The feedback form. Completed in the room (paper or QR), before goodbyes — this single discipline is the difference between real measurement and vibes.
  4. The closing script. Dorina's 60-second close — the "keep the card, use the Friday 15, and know the pathway" arc — is scripted verbatim in WORKSHOP_EXERCISES_AND_HANDOUTS.md so quality never depends on end-of-day energy. It ends with thanks, on time, in whichever language the room's heart was in — usually Arabic: "اعتنوا بحالكن — هيدي مش رفاهية، هيدي شغل" ("take care of yourselves — that's not a luxury, that's part of the work").

8. Measurement

8.1 In-room feedback form (every session)

  1. Overall, how valuable was this session? (1–5)
  2. "I can name my own early stress signals." (1–5, agree)
  3. "I know one concrete thing I will do differently this week." (1–5, agree)
  4. "The facilitator created a comfortable, pressure-free space." (1–5, agree)
  5. How likely are you to recommend this session? (0–10)
  6. The most useful part was… / One thing to improve is… (open)

8.2 The metrics that actually matter

Metric Definition Quality bar (first year, to calibrate)
Session value score Mean of Q1 ≥ 4.2/5
Safety score Mean of Q4 ≥ 4.5/5 — this one is non-negotiable; below 4.5 triggers immediate review of container delivery
Recommendation score Mean of Q5 (0–10) ≥ 8
Behavior echo % of 1-month survey respondents reporting they used ≥1 tool (survey Q3 ≥ 4, or Q4 = yes) ≥ 40% of respondents
Pilot-to-paid conversion % of W0 deliveries that produce a paid booking (any W1–W5) within 90 days ≥ 1 in 3
Rebooking rate % of paying clients who book again (any format, incl. W5) within 12 months ≥ 30%
Referral bookings % of new inquiries citing a past client or participant Track from month 1; this becomes the leading indicator of the "Trusted → Invited" transition

8.3 The iteration loop (simple, ruthless, monthly)

After every session: same-evening, Dorina writes three lines — what landed, what dragged, what I change next time — and updates the run sheet within 48 hours (one change at a time, so cause and effect stay visible). Monthly: review the metrics table; any metric below bar for two consecutive engagements gets a named fix in the next delivery. Quarterly: retire the weakest exercise variant, promote the strongest, and re-validate prices against actual close rates. The quality bar is deliberately modest in year one — the goal is a truthful baseline, then compounding.
