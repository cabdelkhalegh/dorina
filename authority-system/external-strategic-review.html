External Strategic Review — Dorina Salman Mental Health Management Authority Package

Scope of this review: I assessed the package through the consolidated review brief, which includes the full deliverable list, section headings, and representative excerpts from all 15 documents. A handful of findings below concern internal consistency and completeness that can only be confirmed against the full-text files; I've flagged those explicitly as "verify." Everything else is judged on the brief as written.

1. Overall verdict

Strong package, not yet sendable. Conditional pass — ship after the Critical and High fixes below.

This is genuinely premium, execution-ready work. The central strategic decision — building authority on an explicit, binding non-clinical boundary and treating that boundary as the product rather than a limitation — is both ethically right and commercially astute, and it is carried consistently across every document. The positioning wedge (administrative-systems craft applied to workplace wellbeing) occupies real white space. The operational depth (run sheets, verbatim scripts, handouts, objection handling, approval gates) is well beyond typical strategy decks.

It is held back from "send" status by a small number of issues that are disproportionately important because of the subject matter: safety-critical facts that must be verified (crisis numbers, Arabic crisis copy), an internal-tooling name that leaks in a sendable artifact, a core biographical claim that may be factually wrong, and a few coherence/integrity gaps that a careful recipient will notice. None are structural; all are fixable in a focused editing pass.

2. Issues by severity

Critical — do not send until resolved

C1. Verify every crisis and emergency number before this touches a real person. The referral and crisis scripts hard-code Lebanese contact points (a national support line and an emergency number). In a mental-health context, a wrong or out-of-date number in a crisis script is a direct safety harm, not a typo. Confirm the current national emotional-support/suicide-prevention line, its operating hours (if it is not 24/7, the crisis script's "can we call together right now?" needs a fallback), and the correct emergency number for a medical emergency versus general emergency. Treat this as a blocking, independently-verified item.

C2. The review deliverable's filename leaks internal tooling. The fifteenth document (the "external review" file) is currently named after the internal review tool/model, and its headers likely repeat it. This directly violates the package's own scrub rule and would expose internal tooling the moment the folder or portal is shared. Rename it (e.g., External Strategic Review), strip the tool/model name from its headers, and update every reference to it in the README, index page, and ZIP.

C3. Run a complete forbidden-terms scrub across all files, the HTML portal, and the ZIP. The leaked filename in C2 is proof the scrub is not finished. Do a full automated sweep for every term on the brief's exclusion list before packaging. This is a two-minute check that prevents an embarrassing and trust-damaging leak.

C4. Confirm the core employment facts before publishing the profile and story. The entire brand narrative rests on "twelve years behind the executive desk" — it anchors the headline, the About section, a flagship long-form article title, and the proposal's credibility case. The brief itself notes this conflicts with the stated current role (Administrator at a marketing company) and the "Feb 2014–Present" law-firm entry. Publishing an inaccurate work history on a public profile undermines the very authority the package is building. Lock down: current employer, whether the law-firm role has ended, and the honest way to phrase tenure. This also raises C4b: the current-employer conflict — building a public wellbeing brand and soliciting workshops while employed full-time at a marketing firm may breach an employment contract or create a conflict; the "what we still need from Dorina" set should explicitly ask about moonlighting constraints and runway.

C5. Have all Arabic copy reviewed by a native Lebanese-Arabic professional — especially the crisis and referral scripts. Colloquial tone, register, and gendered forms in a crisis message are high-stakes; a near-miss reads as inauthentic or, worse, cold at the exact moment it matters most. The package already shows good instincts (gendered verb forms, "confirm dialect comfort" notes), but the crisis/referral Arabic must be validated by a person, not assumed correct.

High — fix before sending

H1. Resolve the Instagram-timing contradiction. The Instagram engine and the content calendar place the IG launch at Month 7 (~February–March 2027) behind hard launch gates, but the README reading-order and the Prompt Library's Instagram section label it "Month 7+." Pick one (the gated Month-7 logic is the well-argued version) and correct the others. Contradictions like this puncture the "premium, coherent" impression.

H2. Verify the safety-gate prompts actually exist in the Prompt Library. The approval workflow and the library's own instructions depend on a specific automated boundary/fact-check prompt and a QA prompt, referenced by number/section. The inventory in the brief only shows Parts A–E (through the Instagram prompts) and no corresponding section. The safety workflow cannot cite a control that isn't present. Confirm those prompts — and the graphics/automation prompts the introduction promises — are actually written and correctly numbered, or fix the references.

H3. Correct the Ramadan 2027 scheduling. The flagship "Ramadan-aware" module sits in Month 7 (March 2027), but Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin in early-to-mid February 2027 (subject to local sighting) and to run mostly through February into early March. As placed, the primary module lands largely after Ramadan for that year. Center it on Month 6 (February), keep March as the tail, and retain the excellent "confirm by local sighting each year" rule.

H4. Fix dangling and incorrect internal cross-references. The marketing document points to a file that isn't in the package (a "LinkedIn profile optimization" file that is actually the LinkedIn Authority Engine), hedged with "if in package." For a premium deliverable, run a full internal link audit so every cross-reference resolves.

H5. Add the pre-launch safety and legal prerequisites the package currently omits. Three gaps that matter precisely because the work is emotionally charged: (a) professional/public liability insurance for running workshops — check availability and cost in Lebanon; (b) local legal review of the disclaimers, the trademark claim, and the employment/moonlighting question — the package should not assert legal protection as settled fact (see M4); (c) a lightweight, non-clinical credential/training track — e.g., Psychological First Aid or Mental Health First Aid for lay facilitators, plus a facilitation certificate. This would materially harden both the authority claim and the safety posture for someone with no facilitation or mental-health-adjacent training running rooms where disclosures will happen. Frame it strictly as literacy/facilitation training, never as clinical practice.

H6. State an honest total time budget. "~2–3 hours/week" recurs as if it covers the whole engine, but it really covers content production. Add daily engagement, weekly marketing review, workshop prep and delivery, and the approval gates, and the real figure is meaningfully higher — an important reality for someone employed full-time. A one-line weekly-time table prevents an early credibility gap between plan and lived experience.

Medium — should fix

M1. Trademark reality-check on the signature framework. The framework is used pervasively with a symbol. The term is descriptive for desk/office wellbeing, may already be in use, and has had no availability search. Before using in public materials, do a name search in Lebanon and any target markets; otherwise drop the or footnote it as "pending." An unexamined trademark claim on public documents is a small but real overreach.

M2. Recalibrate growth KPIs as ambitious/stretch. Going from ~4 to 1,500+ connections and 2,500+ followers in 12 months for a brand-new niche voice is optimistic; LinkedIn's invitation throttles (roughly ~100/week, often lower) cap the connection pace regardless of effort. Keep the targets as stretch goals, label them as such, and add a floor/expected band so early months don't read as failure.

M3. Pressure-test the Lebanon monetization assumptions. Given the economic environment, domestic corporate training budgets are thin and volatile. Ensure the "conservative" revenue model does not over-index on paid domestic corporate workshops; the diaspora/GCC remote offering and USD-"fresh" pricing are likely the real year-one revenue engine. The Opportunity Map's constraints panel is the right place to make this explicit.

M4. Soften legal overclaims. The summary states the boundary "reduces legal/reputational risk when reviewed with local counsel." Downgrade to risk-reduction language and defer to local counsel. A package this careful about not overclaiming clinical outcomes should apply the same discipline to legal claims about itself.

M5. Add a safeguarding log to the metrics. KPIs are growth- and revenue-weighted. For this subject matter, also track a small safeguarding set — referrals made, protocol adherence, any crisis-adjacent events — so the ethical commitments are measured, not just asserted.

M6. De-emphasize "manage your mental health" in public hero copy. The package does careful work to redefine "mental health management" as non-clinical self-management, but the phrase still reads as quasi-clinical to a lay audience. Lead public-facing copy with "workplace wellbeing" and "mental health literacy"; reserve "mental health management" for the internal, carefully-scoped framing where it's already well-defined.

M7. Reconcile the README with the full deliverable set. The reading-order table lists 13 documents; the package contains 15. Decide intentionally whether the external-review file and the HTML portal appear in the reading order, and make the counts consistent.

Low — polish

L1. Name the other major Lebanese seasonal moments explicitly in the seasonal spine — Christmas and Easter (Lebanon's mixed calendar), Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, and Independence Day (Nov 22) — rather than folding them into generic "year-end."

L2. Avoid stacking the Instagram launch, the highest-Arabic-content push, and the Ramadan module in the same month; that's a heavy load during a lower-energy period. Consider sequencing.

L3. Keep "emotional organization" tightly tethered to the administrative-systems metaphor so it doesn't drift into reading like emotion-regulation therapy.

L4. Fold name-spelling confirmation and the "implied third language" (don't fabricate it) into the same fact-confirmation pass as C4.

3. Required edits before sending (gating checklist)

  1. Verify and, if needed, correct all crisis/emergency numbers and helpline hours; add a fallback in the crisis script. (C1)
  2. Rename the external-review file, strip the internal tool/model name from it, and update all links. (C2)
  3. Run a full forbidden-terms scrub across every file, the HTML portal, and the ZIP. (C3)
  4. Confirm employment facts and current-employer constraints; fix the "12 years / executive desk" narrative to match reality before any profile goes live. (C4)
  5. Native Lebanese-Arabic review of all Arabic copy, crisis/referral scripts first. (C5)
  6. Reconcile the Instagram launch timing to one month across all documents. (H1)
  7. Confirm the referenced safety-check and QA prompts (and the promised graphics/automation prompts) actually exist and are correctly numbered. (H2)
  8. Move the Ramadan module to February 2027 as primary; keep the annual-confirmation rule. (H3)
  9. Audit and fix all internal cross-references. (H4)
  10. Add the insurance, local legal review, and non-clinical training prerequisites; add an honest total-time budget. (H5, H6)

Items 1–5 are true blockers. Items 6–10 should be done in the same pass so the package goes out coherent and complete.

4. Strengths (keep these intact)

  • The non-clinical boundary as strategy, not disclaimer. Making the credential gap the foundation of credibility — and encoding it in a binding protocol that explicitly supersedes every other document — is the package's best idea. It is ethically sound and commercially smart: it lowers the liability friction that would otherwise make companies hesitate to book.
  • Disciplined evidence base. Grounding in WHO and Lebanon's national programme, with strict attribution and a "don't invent data" rule, gives the authority claim a real spine and models the behavior the brand needs.
  • Safety-forward operations. The approval gates (human sign-off on both text and graphic, no autopost, a STOP protocol), the tiered red-flag system, and crisis scripts that never promise secrecy and hand over rather than treat are responsible and rehearsable.
  • Genuinely differentiated positioning. "Administrative systems × wellbeing" and "emotional organization" is a distinct archetype in a field crowded with clinicians and motivational speakers — credible without a clinical license and aligned to how work actually breaks people.
  • Execution-ready, not aspirational. Verbatim run sheets, exercises, handouts, DM and objection scripts, feedback forms, and phased pilots-before-paid mean the recipient can start within weeks, not re-plan for months.
  • Local and bilingual credibility. Arabic delivery, Ramadan sensitivity, and alignment with (rather than competition against) the national ecosystem make this land as Lebanon-native.
  • Good consulting hygiene. The recurring "what we still need from Dorina" sections surface open questions without blocking the plan — exactly right for a package that depends on facts only she can confirm.

Bottom line: the thinking is sound and the craft is high. Clear the five Critical items and the five High-priority edits, and this is a confidently sendable, premium package.

Improvements Incorporated After Review

The following fixes were applied before packaging:

  1. The external-review file was renamed to a recipient-safe title and internal model/tool language was removed from the sendable package.
  2. Instagram timing was reconciled to Month 7+, after LinkedIn is stable and approval workflow is working.
  3. Ramadan-aware content was corrected to a February–March 2027 window, with annual confirmation required because Ramadan dates shift by lunar sighting.
  4. Legal language was softened from “protects legally” to “reduces risk”; Dorina is instructed to seek local legal/employment review before paid workshops.
  5. The signature framework was changed from a trademark-style label to a proposed brand framework pending name/trademark checks.
  6. Non-clinical prerequisites were made explicit: Psychological First Aid or comparable mental-health-literacy training, facilitation practice, referral map verification, and insurance/legal checks before paid delivery.
  7. The time budget was clarified: content alone can be 2–3 hours/week, but the full authority engine requires a larger weekly operating rhythm.
  8. A safeguarding metrics layer was added: referrals made, boundary events, workshop stop/debrief notes, and protocol adherence are tracked alongside growth KPIs.
  9. Crisis information was narrowed to source-grounded wording and a verification checkpoint: Embrace Lebanon publicly lists 1564 for the Lifeline; all crisis/emergency numbers and operating hours must be re-verified before printing or delivery.
  10. Generic references to “nonprofit/humanitarian organization” were changed to “nonprofit/humanitarian organizations” to avoid confusing the package with any unrelated project.

Remaining Human Checks Before Public Use

These cannot be solved by documentation alone and must be confirmed by Dorina or a qualified local reviewer:

  • Dorina’s exact current employment facts, dates, and any employer moonlighting/conflict restrictions.
  • Native Lebanese-Arabic review of all public Arabic copy, especially referral and crisis scripts.
  • Local legal review of disclaimers, workshop terms, privacy boundaries, and paid-service structure.
  • Current official crisis/emergency contacts and hours immediately before printing handouts or running workshops.
  • Dorina’s actual completed training/certificates; no credential wording goes public until completed.
